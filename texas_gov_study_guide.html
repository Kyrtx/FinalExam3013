The Legislature in Texas

Structure & Sessions

▼

Texas has a bicameral legislature — two chambers, modeled after Congress:

  • Texas House of Representatives — 150 members, 2-year terms
  • Texas Senate — 31 members, 4-year terms

Both chambers use single-member districts (one representative per district). A Texas state senator represents more people than a U.S. House member because there are only 31 senators for the entire state.

Why bicameralism matters: Before any law passes, both chambers must approve it. This forces compromise but can slow things down. If a bill can't be killed in one chamber, it can be killed or changed in the other.

Sessions:

  • Regular session — 140 days every odd-numbered year (biennial)
  • Special sessions — Called only by the governor, last up to 30 days, and only the governor sets the agenda

Compensation: Legislators are paid very little — essentially a part-time salary with a daily allowance (per diem). Most have full-time jobs outside the legislature. This low pay tends to attract people from higher socioeconomic backgrounds.

Who serves? The legislature is disproportionately White, male, and highly educated compared to the state population, but women and minorities have been increasing their representation, especially in the House.

Redistricting

▼

Every 10 years after the census, district lines must be redrawn. This is called redistricting.

  • One-person, one-vote principle: Districts must have roughly equal populations.
  • Redistricting is an intensely partisan activity — the party in power draws lines to benefit itself (gerrymandering).
  • Plans must comply with the Voting Rights Act so minority voters are not disadvantaged.
  • If the legislature fails to redistrict, the Legislative Redistricting Board (LRB) takes over — made up of the lieutenant governor, Speaker, attorney general, land commissioner, and comptroller.
Exam tip: Redistricting shapes who holds power for an entire decade. It's one of the most partisan things the legislature does.

Powers of the Legislature

▼

Legislative Powers: Passing bills (proposed laws). Types include:

  • General bills — Apply statewide
  • Local bills — Affect specific areas
  • Special bills — Give exemptions

The legislature also passes resolutions (expressions of opinion):

  • Simple resolution — One chamber only
  • Concurrent resolution — Both chambers, generally needs governor's signature
  • Joint resolution — Proposed constitutional amendments; do NOT need the governor's signature but DO need voter approval

Nonlegislative Powers:

  • Electoral power — Counting returns for governor/lt. governor elections
  • Investigative power — Investigating problems in state government
  • Directive and supervisory power — Controlling agencies through the budget
  • Judicial power — The House can impeach officials; the Senate holds the trial

How a Bill Becomes Law

▼

Anyone can write a bill, but only legislators can introduce one. Revenue bills must start in the House. The process has six basic steps:

  1. Introduction — The legislator files the bill. It's numbered, enrolled, and read for the first time.
  2. Referral — The Speaker (House) or lieutenant governor (Senate) assigns the bill to a standing committee. Since committees have overlapping jurisdictions, leaders can send it to a friendly or unfriendly committee — a major source of power.
  3. Committee Action — Most bills die here. The chair can kill a bill by pigeonholing (setting it aside forever). Surviving bills are usually amended. Public hearings may be held.
  4. Floor Action — In the House, the Calendars Committee schedules debate. In the Senate, debate is unlimited, allowing for filibusters (talking for hours to block a bill). A related tactic is "chubbing" — debating at length to slow everything down near session's end.
  5. Conference Committee — If the House and Senate pass different versions, a conference committee (5 from each chamber) works out a compromise. The report must be accepted or rejected as-is — no amendments allowed.
  6. Governor's Action — The governor can sign, veto, or let the bill become law without a signature.
Key veto concepts:
• Regular veto — Can be overridden by ⅔ of both chambers (extremely rare — hasn't happened since 1979)
• Post-adjournment veto — Vetoing after the legislature goes home; essentially absolute because there's no legislature to override it
• Line-item veto — Striking specific spending items from the budget while signing the rest. The president does NOT have this power.

Leadership & Power

▼

Speaker of the House

Elected by House members (can involve bipartisan coalitions). Assigns bills to committees, appoints committee chairs/members, controls floor debate through recognition, and appoints conference committee members.

Lieutenant Governor

Elected statewide, separately from the governor. Presides over the Senate. Assigns bills to committees, appoints chairs/members, and has recognition power. Often called the most powerful official in Texas.

Sources of centralized power: Committee assignments (reward allies, punish opponents), ability to speed up or block legislation, and appointment of conference committee members giving influence over final bill language.

Partisanship

▼

The Texas legislature has become increasingly partisan, mirroring the U.S. Congress. Republicans control both chambers but have internal divisions between establishment/moderate and Tea Party-style conservatives. Democrats have gained ground in urban areas but Republicans remain firmly in control.

The elimination of the two-thirds rule in the Senate (which previously required bipartisan cooperation to bring bills to the floor) has accelerated partisanship. Now only a simple majority is needed to move legislation forward, meaning the minority party can be shut out entirely.

The Executive Branch in Texas

The Governor — A "Weak" Governor

▼

Texas intentionally created a weak governor. This stems from the 1876 Constitution, written after Reconstruction, when Texans deeply distrusted concentrated executive power. Texas's governor ranks among the weakest in the country in institutional power.

Qualifications: At least 30 years old, U.S. citizen, lived in Texas for at least five years.

Term: Four-year terms, no term limits. Elections in off-years (not presidential election years) to prevent the presidential race from influencing the governor's race.

Removal: Through impeachment (House charges, Senate tries and can convict).

Succession: If the governor can't serve, the lieutenant governor takes over. If the governor leaves the state, the lt. governor becomes acting governor.

Governor's Powers

▼

Executive Powers:

  • Appointment power — The governor's MOST significant power. Appoints members to 400+ boards/commissions that run state agencies. The Senate must confirm, and senatorial courtesy (informal practice where the appointee's home senator must approve) effectively gives senators a veto.
  • Budgetary power — Submits an executive budget suggesting priorities, but the legislative budget (prepared by the LBB) is the one that actually matters.
  • Military/police power — Can deploy the National Guard and Texas Rangers.

Legislative Powers:

  • Message power — Propose legislation and highlight priorities
  • Veto power — Regular veto, post-adjournment veto (absolute), and line-item veto on spending
  • Special sessions — Only the governor calls them and sets the agenda; can last up to 30 days

Judicial Powers:

  • Can grant one 30-day reprieve from execution per condemned person. All other clemency must be recommended by the Board of Pardons and Paroles — the governor cannot act alone.
Exam tip: The appointment power is the governor's most important tool. The line-item veto and special sessions are the governor's strongest legislative bargaining chips. But overall, the Texas governor is considered weak compared to governors in other states.

The Plural Executive

▼

Unlike the president (who appoints cabinet members), Texans elect most executive officials independently. This is the plural executive — power is fragmented so no single person controls the branch.

  • Lieutenant Governor — Elected statewide, separately from the governor. Presides over the Senate. Often the most powerful official in Texas.
  • Attorney General — The state's chief lawyer. Issues legal opinions that carry the weight of law unless overturned by a court.
  • Comptroller of Public Accounts — Chief tax collector and financial officer. Critically, the comptroller certifies the budget — confirming projected revenue covers spending. No certification = legislature must adjust.
  • Commissioner of the General Land Office — Manages publicly owned lands and mineral rights; awards oil/gas exploration rights.
  • Agriculture Commissioner — Enforces agricultural laws.
  • Secretary of State — The ONLY member appointed by the governor (confirmed by Senate). Administers elections.
Why this matters: Because these officials are elected independently, they have their own power bases, don't answer to the governor, and may even be political rivals. This disperses power but can create fragmentation and lack of coordination.

Boards, Commissions & Regulatory Agencies

▼

Much of Texas government is run by boards and commissions whose members are appointed by the governor. These include multimember appointed boards, appointed single executives, and multimember elected boards (like the Railroad Commission and the State Board of Education).

The Sunset Advisory Commission (SAC), created in 1977, periodically reviews state agencies to determine if they're still needed. Agencies can be abolished if they're found to be ineffective. This is how the legislature holds agencies accountable.

The Judiciary in the US and Texas

Civil Law vs. Criminal Law

▼

Civil Law

Disputes between individuals or organizations (contracts, property, personal injury, family law). Remedy is usually money. Standard: "preponderance of the evidence" (more likely than not).

Criminal Law

Offenses against society/state. Felonies = serious (prison). Misdemeanors = less serious (fine/jail). Standard: "beyond a reasonable doubt" (much higher).

Key criminal concepts:

  • Grand jury — Decides if there's enough evidence to formally charge someone (issue an indictment). Does NOT decide guilt or innocence.
  • Plea bargaining — The vast majority of criminal cases are resolved through plea deals, not trials.

Texas Court Structure

▼

Texas has a large, complex, and sometimes confusing court system with overlapping jurisdictions.

Trial Courts (where cases start):

  • Justice of the Peace Courts — Small claims, minor criminal, traffic. JPs are NOT required to be lawyers.
  • Municipal Courts — City ordinance violations and minor criminal.
  • Constitutional County Courts — Presided over by the county judge.
  • Statutory County Courts — Created by the legislature to handle caseloads.
  • District Courts — Main trial courts for felonies and major civil cases. The workhorses of the system.

Appellate Courts:

  • Courts of Appeals — 14 intermediate appellate courts across the state.
  • Texas Supreme Court — Highest court for civil cases only.
  • Texas Court of Criminal Appeals — Highest court for criminal cases only.
Exam tip: Texas is unusual in having two highest courts — one for civil and one for criminal. Most states have just one supreme court. The system has been criticized as a "patchwork" of overlapping jurisdictions.

Judicial Politics — How Judges Are Selected

▼

Federal Judges

Appointed by the president, confirmed by the Senate, serve for life. Insulated from political pressure.

Texas Judges

Elected in partisan elections — they run as Democrats or Republicans. Must eventually face voters even if initially appointed.

Problems with partisan judicial elections:

  • Voters usually know very little about judicial candidates and vote on party label or name recognition alone.
  • Urban counties can have dozens of judicial races on the ballot — impossible for voters to be informed on all of them.
  • Straight-ticket voting historically swept judges in or out purely on party popularity (banned starting 2020).
  • Judicial campaigns are expensive; most money comes from lawyers and interest groups who appear before these judges — raising serious impartiality concerns.

Proposed reforms:

  • Merit selection — A commission screens candidates, the governor appoints, and after a period in office, the judge faces a retention election (yes/no — no opponent). Proposed many times but never adopted.
  • The Judicial Campaign Fairness Act places limits on campaign contributions to judicial candidates.

Key Issues in the Court System

▼
  • Tort reform — Texas has passed laws limiting damages in civil lawsuits, especially medical malpractice. A battleground between business interests and plaintiffs' lawyers.
  • Diversity — Judges have been disproportionately White and male. Urban elections in 2018 and 2020 brought more minority judges, especially in Harris County.
  • Judicial conduct — The State Commission on Judicial Conduct investigates complaints and issues sanctions.
  • Structural inefficiency — Overlapping jurisdictions, courts with the same name having different responsibilities, and no comprehensive reform despite decades of proposals.

Interest Groups & Lobbying

What Are Interest Groups?

▼

An interest group is an organization of people who share a common interest and band together to influence public policy. They are NOT political parties — they don't run candidates. Instead, they try to influence the people who ARE in government.

Key resources:

  • Members — More people (and more influential people) = more clout.
  • Money — Ability to raise and spend money on campaigns and lobbying.
  • Information/Expertise — Interest groups often provide research and data to legislators. First drafts of many bills actually come from interest groups.

Four main strategies:

  1. Political — Grassroots organizing, get-out-the-vote, campaign financing
  2. Legislative — Lobbying legislators, testifying before committees
  3. Public awareness — Ads, reports, press conferences
  4. Judicial — Filing or supporting lawsuits

Pluralism is the idea that many competing groups can check and balance each other. But the free rider problem makes organizing harder — people benefit from a group's work without joining or contributing.

Types of Interest Groups

▼
  • Business groups — Most powerful in Texas (e.g., Texas Association of Business, oil/gas industry)
  • Professional groups — Represent specific professions (Texas Medical Association, Texas Bar Association)
  • Public employee groups — Teachers, firefighters, police officers
  • Single-issue groups — Focused on one topic (abortion, environment, guns)
  • Civil rights groups — NAACP, LULAC, MALDEF
  • Public interest groups — Broader causes like consumer protection (Sierra Club, Public Citizen, Common Cause). Usually less money but can mobilize members.
  • Ideological groups — Like ALEC, a conservative group that drafts model legislation for states nationwide
Exam tip: Business interests dominate Texas lobbying. A persistent criticism is that there is virtually no counterbalancing lobby for ordinary consumers, the environment, or social services.

Lobbyists & Access

▼

A lobbyist is a person hired by an interest group to influence government decisions. They gain access through entertainment (dinners, events), personal relationships, providing information, and mobilizing constituents.

The Texas Ethics Commission registers lobbyists and tracks spending. Despite regulations, Texas's lobbying laws are considered relatively loose.

The Revolving Door: Former legislators increasingly become lobbyists after leaving office, using their relationships and insider knowledge. Proposals for "cooling off" periods have repeatedly failed.

Corruption concerns: Lobbying scandals have led to reforms (the Ethics Commission was created after scandals), but entertainment of legislators and use of campaign contributions for personal expenses remain controversial.

PACs & Elections

▼

Political Action Committees (PACs) are created by interest groups to collect and distribute campaign contributions. Bundling combines many small contributions into larger, more impactful donations.

Interest groups also engage in getting out the vote for favored candidates and working to defeat opponents through negative advertising or supporting challengers. Business interests dominate PAC contributions in Texas.

Local Government

County Government

▼

Texas has 254 counties — more than any other state. Counties primarily govern rural areas and function largely as administrative arms of the state. Unlike cities, they have very limited self-government powers and cannot adopt home-rule charters.

Functions: Roads/bridges, law enforcement, dispute resolution, record-keeping, and social services.

Key officials:

  • County Judge — Presides over both the commissioners' court (governing body) AND the constitutional county court. This is both an executive/legislative and judicial role.
  • Commissioners' Court — County judge + 4 commissioners (elected from precincts). Sets budget, tax rate, and oversees operations.
  • Sheriff — Chief law enforcement officer.
  • County/District Attorney — Prosecutes criminal cases.

City Government

▼

General-Law Cities

Smaller cities that can only do what the state legislature specifically allows.

Home-Rule Cities

Population over 5,000, adopt their own charter, broad self-governing authority. But the state can preempt (overrule) local ordinances.

Preemption has become a major issue — the state legislature increasingly blocks cities from passing their own regulations on things like fracking, plastic bags, etc.

Three forms of city government:

  1. Mayor-Council — Mayor as chief executive, council as legislature. Strong mayor = lots of power (appointments, veto, agenda). Weak mayor = power more diffused.
  2. Commissioner — Small elected commission runs the city. Rare today.
  3. Council-Manager — Most common in Texas home-rule cities. Council sets policy, a professional city manager handles day-to-day administration. Mayor has limited power.

Special Purpose Districts

▼

A special purpose district performs one specific function in a limited geographic area. Texas has thousands.

Two types:

  • School districts — Every piece of land is in one. Governed by elected boards of trustees who hire a superintendent, set the budget, and set tax rates.
  • Nonschool special districts — Hospital districts, water districts, fire protection, etc. Most notable: Municipal Utility Districts (MUDs) providing water/sewer/utilities outside city limits, especially around Houston.

Funded by: Property taxes and user fees.

Problems:

  • Hidden governments — Most people don't know they exist or vote in their elections (under 10% turnout).
  • Developer abuses — Developers create districts, have a tiny number of "residents" approve millions in bonds, then leave future homeowners to pay.
  • Lack of coordination — Thousands of overlapping districts make coherent statewide policy nearly impossible.

Councils of Government (COGs) — 24 regional planning bodies that coordinate across local government boundaries for major projects. They don't tax — they plan and coordinate.

Financial Issues Facing Local Government

▼
  • Capital Appreciation Bonds (CABs) — Local governments (usually school districts) borrow money now but pay nothing until the bond matures far in the future. Total payback can be enormously more than what was borrowed. Used to get around borrowing limits.
  • Deferred Retirement Option Plans (DROPs) — Generous local government pensions that create significant long-term financial obligations straining local budgets.

Public Policy in Texas

The Policy-Making Process

▼

Public policy = the outputs of government (laws, regulations, spending). The process has stages:

  1. Problem identification / agenda setting — An issue is recognized as needing government action
  2. Policy formulation — Possible solutions are developed
  3. Policy adoption — A solution is enacted into law
  4. Policy implementation — Carried out by agencies
  5. Policy evaluation — Effectiveness is assessed, leading to changes

Rationality in policy making is the ideal — clearly identifying goals and finding the most efficient solution. In reality, policy making is messy, political, and influenced by competing interests, limited information, and short-term thinking.

Education Policy

▼

Education is one of the biggest budget items. Key themes:

  • Equity in Funding — Funded through state money + local property taxes + federal funds. Wealthier districts raise more property tax revenue, creating significant inequality. Multiple lawsuits have challenged this, arguing it violates the Texas Constitution's requirement for an "efficient" system.
  • Accountability & Testing — Texas has been a leader in standardized testing to hold schools accountable.
  • Charter Schools — Receive public funding but operate independently. Controversial: supporters say choice and innovation, critics say they drain resources from traditional schools.
  • School Finance Reform — An ongoing battle between adequate funding, property tax relief, and equity between districts.

Welfare Policy

▼

Poverty rates in Texas are above the national average, especially among children and the elderly. Texas's individualistic culture tends to view poverty as an individual problem.

Key policy evolution:

  • The Social Security Act of 1935 (New Deal) created the modern welfare system with shared state-federal responsibility.
  • Great Society programs in the 1960s expanded anti-poverty programs.
  • 1990s welfare reform reflected a belief that earlier programs created "dependency." Replaced the old program (AFDC) with Temporary Assistance for Needy Families (TANF), imposing time limits, work requirements, and more state flexibility.

Medicaid & Health Care

▼

Texas has the highest rate of uninsured people of any state — far above the national average.

Medicaid is a joint state-federal program providing health care for the poor, especially children. It's one of the most expensive budget items. Federal money comes with strings — Texas must meet standards to receive funding.

The Affordable Care Act (ACA) expanded Medicaid eligibility and created insurance marketplaces. However, Texas refused to expand Medicaid, leaving many poor Texans without coverage. Texas also didn't create its own marketplace.

Exam tip: Texas's refusal to expand Medicaid is one of the most consequential policy decisions discussed in this chapter. It leaves the state with the highest uninsured rate in the nation and is a defining example of Texas's "low service" philosophy.

Public Finance in Texas

The Budget

▼

The Texas Constitution requires a balanced budget — projected revenue must equal or exceed projected spending.

Five revenue streams:

  1. General Revenue Funds — Main operating fund, most taxes/fees flow here. Legislators have the most control over this.
  2. General Revenue–Dedicated Funds — Money targeted for specific purposes.
  3. Federal Funds — Over a third of all state revenue. Comes with strings attached.
  4. Other Funds — Highway Fund, trust funds, higher education accounts.
  5. All Funds — The total of everything above.

Key points: Much money is outside legislators' direct control. Education and health/human services are the two biggest spending categories. Federal dollars play a huge and growing role.

Revenue & Taxes

▼

Texas is a "low service, low tax" state and does NOT have a personal income tax. A constitutional amendment requires voter approval for any income tax, with revenue going to public education.

Major revenue sources:

  • Sales tax — Largest source. State rate: 6.25%; local additions up to 2% more (max 8.25%).
  • Property taxes — Among the highest in the nation. Levied by local governments, not the state.
  • Oil/gas severance taxes — Still important but less dominant than historically.
  • Other — Motor fuels tax, franchise tax (on businesses), cigarette/alcohol/hotel taxes, motor vehicle sales taxes.
  • Federal money — The single largest revenue source overall, primarily for Medicaid and education.

Progressive Tax

Takes a larger percentage from higher-income people (like an income tax).

Regressive Tax

Takes a larger percentage from lower-income people (like a sales tax — same rate, bigger burden on those with less).

Exam tip: Texas's tax system is considered regressive because it relies on sales and property taxes instead of income taxes. Lower-income Texans pay a higher share of their income in taxes.

Important State Funds

▼
  • Permanent School Fund (PSF) — One of the largest educational endowments in the country. Funds public schools from land and mineral rights revenue.
  • Permanent University Fund (PUF) — Supports UT and Texas A&M systems through oil/gas revenue from public lands.
  • State Highway Fund — Motor fuels taxes + additional sales tax revenue (constitutional amendment approved by voters).
  • Economic Stabilization Fund ("Rainy Day Fund") — Funded by oil/gas severance taxes. Cushions the budget during downturns. Requires a supermajority vote to tap.

The Budgetary Process

▼

Key players:

  • Legislative Budget Board (LBB) — The most important budget body. 10 members, chaired by the lt. governor, Speaker as vice chair. Prepares the budget the legislature actually uses (the governor's budget is largely symbolic).
  • Governor's Office of Budget, Planning, and Policy (GOBPP) — Develops the executive budget and exercises the line-item veto after passage.
  • Comptroller of Public Accounts — Issues revenue estimates and certifies the final budget (confirms it's balanced). If the comptroller won't certify, the legislature must adjust.

The process:

  1. Estimation/Planning — LBB gathers requests from agencies, develops draft budget.
  2. Legislative Process — Budget moves through House Appropriations and Senate Finance committees separately. Conference committee reconciles differences.
  3. Certification — Comptroller certifies balance.
  4. Governor's Action — Sign, veto, or line-item veto.
  5. Implementation — Takes effect September 1 of odd-numbered years. LBB and state auditor monitor compliance.
Remember: Passing the budget is the most important thing the legislature does. As long as conservative Republicans control Texas, cutting taxes stays high on the agenda while expanding social programs does not. Texas's economy (especially oil prices) creates dramatic revenue swings that make budgeting especially challenging.

These overarching ideas connect all the chapters. Great for last-minute review.

01
Fragmented power is deliberate. The weak governor, plural executive, bicameral legislature, and elected judges all reflect distrust of concentrated power dating to the 1876 Constitution.
02
The Speaker and Lt. Governor are enormously powerful — arguably more than the governor — because they control the legislative process through committee assignments, recognition, and conference committee appointments.
03
Texas is a "low tax, low service" state. No income tax, heavy reliance on regressive sales and property taxes, and below-average spending on education and social services.
04
Partisan elections for judges create unique problems. Low voter knowledge, money from lawyers/interest groups, and party sweeps can remove experienced judges based purely on party label.
05
Business interests dominate Texas politics. There is little counterbalancing lobby for consumers, the environment, or the poor. The revolving door reinforces this dynamic.
06
Local government is complex and fragmented. Thousands of special purpose districts operate as "hidden governments" with minimal voter participation. Cities face increasing state preemption.
07
The budget is the most important legislative task. Understanding who controls it (LBB, comptroller, governor's line-item veto) is essential to understanding Texas politics.
08
Texas's refusal to expand Medicaid leaves it with the highest uninsured rate in the nation — a defining policy choice that reflects the state's conservative philosophy.
09
Redistricting is intensely partisan and shapes who holds power for a decade. The party controlling the legislature draws lines to benefit itself.
10
Texas is changing demographically — more diverse, more urban, more politically competitive — creating tensions within and between parties and putting pressure on longstanding institutions.
